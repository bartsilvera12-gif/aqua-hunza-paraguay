Start Thinking: Water for Livestock

Livestock dry matter intake is directly related to the water they consume.

← Volver al blog

Studies have consistently shown that livestock dry matter intake is related to water consumed. The more dry matter consumed, the more weight calves can put on and the more milk the cow can give. Installing a sufficient water system using troughs, ponds or other means is key to a good grazing system.

The importance of water in livestock performance

60–80 percent of the animal’s live body weight is water. It helps eliminate waste products of digestion and metabolism, regulates blood osmotic pressure, helps produce milk and saliva, transports nutrients and hormones, and aids temperature regulation.

Limiting water intake will reduce animal performance quicker and more drastically than any other nutrient deficiency.

Water temperature and animal health

Access to cool, clean drinking water is essential to keep an animal’s internal body temperature within normal limits. As water temperature increases from 70 °F to 95 °F, total water requirements for each animal increase by about 2.5 times.

Drinking space requirements

For group watering, tanks should hold at least 25 percent of the daily herd requirement, with 5–10 percent of animals drinking simultaneously at two feet per head. Example: 40 head × 10% = 4 head at a time × 2′ = an 8′ trough (one side). Tank refill time should not exceed one hour.

Water quality and weight gain

  • University of Florida: heifers drinking from a well or spring gained 23 percent more weight than heifers drinking pond water.
  • Mississippi State: 9 percent higher weight gain in nursing calves using troughs versus ponds, and 16–19 percent increase for steers.
  • Montana State: 76 percent of beef cattle preferred troughs over pond water.
  • Livestock drinking from a trough have less risk of contracting illness compared to a pond.

Algae growth and control

Algae growth is driven by nutrients from runoff, decaying plant material and fecal contamination. Blue-green algae (cyanobacteria) can multiply rapidly and a few species occasionally produce toxins known to harm animals.

  • Periodically clean the tank to reduce nutrients and slow algae growth.
  • Apply copper sulfate crystals every 2–4 weeks as needed (dissolve in warm water first).
  • Chlorine bleach is another option: 2–3 ounces per 50 gallons of capacity.
  • Note: copper sulfate may deteriorate metal pipes, and some livestock (e.g. sheep) cannot tolerate high copper levels.

Good quality, accessible water is a commodity you never have too much of.
